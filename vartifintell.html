So for decades from the 1950s through the 1990s, the field of natural language processing, NLP, as everyone calls it, was split between two camps that didn't much like each other. On one side were the rationalists, the rule-based people, the ones who believed that understanding language meant understanding grammar and meaning and logic. They built elaborate systems with handcrafted rules and carefully structured knowledge representations. The most famous of these was probably Noom Chsky, the MIT linguist who revolutionized the field in the late 1950s with his theory of transformational grammar. Chsky argued that language has a deep structure, a universal grammar hardwired into the human brain and that the job of linguistics was to discover the rules of that grammar. His ideas were enormously influential and they reinforced the belief that language was fundamentally about rules, about structure, about formal systems that could in principle be written down completely. Chomsky's influence cannot be overstated. For decades, he was arguably the most cited scholar in the humanities. And his ideas about the innate structure of language shaped not just linguistics, but cognitive science, philosophy, and psychology. He believed that the ability to learn language was hardwired into the human brain. that children come equipped with a universal grammar, a kind of template for what human languages can look like, and that learning a specific language is a matter of setting the parameters of this template based on what you hear around you. This view, sometimes called linguistic nivism, implied that you couldn't understand language by looking at data alone. You had to understand the innate structure and that meant rule-based theorydriven approaches were the only path to real understanding. On the other side were the empiricists, the statisticians, the ones who believed that the patterns in actual language data were more important than any theory about how language worked.

They counted things. They calculated probabilities. They built models that learned from text rather than from rules. And for a long time, they were the underdogs, dismissed by the linguistic establishment as number crunchers who were missing the point. There's a famous story about this divide that captures the tension perfectly. In the late 1980s, a team of researchers at IBM, there's IBM again, always at the interesting moments, developed a statistical machine translation system that worked by analyzing large collections of parallel text, documents that existed in both French and English, and learning the statistical patterns of how words and phrases in one language corresponded to words and phrases in the other. The statistical revolution in NLP, real as it was, still had a fundamental bottleneck. These models were working with handcrafted features. That dream was still out of reach, and it would stay out of reach until neural networks came to language, which they did, but not until they had solved a problem that turned out to be absolutely fundamental. a problem so basic that you might not even think of it as a problem at all until someone points it out. And that problem is this. How do you represent a word as a number? Think about that for a second because it's deeper than it sounds. Computers work with numbers. Everything a computer does underneath all the abstraction is arithmetic. addition, multiplication, comparison. If you want a computer to do anything with words, you need to turn those words into numbers first. And the obvious way to do that, give each word a number. Like word one is the and word two is cat and word three is sat. That doesn't work at all because in that scheme, cat is number two and dog is say number 4,700. And the number system implies that cat is closer to 'the' than it is to dog, which makes no sense. The numbers are just arbitrary labels. They don't capture any relationship between words. Cat and kitten should be close together somehow. Hot and cold should be related. King and queen should share something. But arbitrary numbering gives you none of that. The standard approach for decades was something called one hot encoding. In one hot encoding, each word is represented by a vector, a list of numbers with one slot for every word in your vocabulary. If your vocabulary has 50,000 words, then each word is a list of 50,000 numbers and every number in that list is zero except for one, which is one. The word cat might be represented as a list where slot number 3,27 is 1 and every other slot is zero. Dog would have a one in a different slot. It's like having a row of 50,000 light switches and to represent any word you flip exactly one of them on. This works technically, but it's fantastically wasteful and more importantly, it's completely flat. Every word is exactly as far from every other word as any other pair. Cat is no closer to kitten than it is to democracy or purple or yodelling. The representation captures nothing about what words mean, how they relate, or how they behave. It's purely symbolic. And if you want a neural network to learn anything useful about language, giving it these flat, meaningless representations as input is like asking someone to learn about animals by staring at their social security numbers. The numbers don't encode any of the relevant information.

The breakthrough, and this is genuinely one of the most beautiful ideas in all of modern AI, was to let the network learn its own representations. Instead of assigning words fixed predetermined vectors, you give each word a vector of say 300 numbers and you start those numbers at random and then you train a neural network on a language task and as the network learns it adjusts those numbers. The word vectors aren't fixed inputs. They're parameters. They're part of what the network learns. And the magic is in what happens to those vectors as training progresses. The idea of learned word representations had been floating around since the early 2000s. Yosua Benio and his colleagues at the University of Montreal published an important paper in 2003 proposing a neural language model that learned word embeddings as part of its training. But the idea didn't really catch fire until 2013 when Tomas Mikolov and his colleagues at Google published word tovec and the world sat up and paid attention. The idea behind word tovec was elegant in its simplicity. You take a huge amount of text, billions of words scraped from the internet, from books, from news articles, and you train a small shallow neural network on one of two simple tasks. Either you give it a word and ask it to predict the surrounding words which is called the skipgram gram approach or you give it the surrounding words and ask it to predict the word in the middle which is called the continuous bag of words approach. That's it. No grammar rules, no semantic annotation, no humanlabeled data, just a massive amount of raw text and a simple prediction task. And what emerged was astonishing as the network learned to predict words from their context. The word vectors, those lists of 300 numbers representing each word, organized themselves in a space that captured meaning. Words that appeared in similar contexts ended up with similar vectors. Cat and kitten ended up close together. King and queen ended up close together. Paris and France ended up close together in the same direction as Berlin and Germany. The network had discovered entirely on its own from raw text that these words are related. And it had encoded those relationships as geometric structure, as directions and distances in a highdimensional space that humans can't visualize, but that mathematics can work with perfectly well. But here's the part that really made people sit up. The part that got researchers genuinely excited and a little unsettled. The relationships weren't just about similarity. They were algebraic. You could do arithmetic with the word vectors and the results made semantic sense. The most famous example, and it really is one of those results that makes you stop and think. Take the vector for king, subtract the vector for man, add the vector for woman, and the resulting vector is closest to the vector for queen. King minus man plus woman equals queen. The network had learned that the relationship between king and queen is the same as the relationship between man and woman, a kind of gender transformation. And it had encoded this as a direction in the vector space. You could do the same thing with country capital relationships. France minus Paris plus Berlin gets you close to Germany. Or verb tenses. Walking minus walk plus swam gets you close to swimming. Think about what that implies for a second. Really sit with it. It means that meaning or at least some important aspect of meaning can be extracted purely from patterns of usage. The model was never told what king means. It was never given a definition of woman. It was never taught the concept of royalty or gender. It just saw these words used in billions of contexts. And from those contexts alone, it figured out how they relate to each other. It discovered an abstract concept, the gender dimension, and encoded it as a direction in a mathematical space. And it did the same thing for hundreds of other conceptual dimensions. Simultaneously, there are directions in the space that correspond to verb tense, to geographic location, to formality of register, to whether something is alive or inanimate, all learned from raw text, all emerging from the statistics of co-occurrence.

The implications were staggering and they rippled out through the entire field of natural language processing almost immediately. After wordtovec, you could just use the word vectors as input features and the downstream model would have access to all the semantic information those vectors encoded. It was like giving every NLP system a free education in the meaning of words. But let's come back to the deeper implication because it matters for everything that follows. What word tovec showed is that meaning or at least some important aspect of meaning can be extracted from patterns of usage. You don't need to define what a word means. You don't need to explain the concept of gender to the algorithm or teach it about countries and capitals. You just need to see how words are used over and over in millions of contexts and the meaning emerges from the pattern. It showed that the company a word keeps the other words that tend to appear near it contains enough information to build a rich structured representation of meaning. And there was something almost philosophical about it. The meaning wasn't programmed in. It wasn't defined by a dictionary or a knowledge base. It emerged from the statistics of co-occurrence. The way a photograph emerges from millions of tiny dots of color. Now word tovec was a landmark. But it had a significant limitation that matters a lot for our story. Each word got exactly one vector regardless of context. The word bank had a single representation whether it appeared in river bank or bank account. But obviously it means different things in those two contexts. A single fixed vector can't capture that. What you really want is a representation of a word that changes depending on what's around it.

A contextual embedding as it came to be called. And getting contextual embeddings would require a different kind of architecture. It would require processing not just individual words but whole sequences of words keeping track of relationships and dependencies across entire sentences and paragraphs. And that brings us to the recurrent neural networks or RNNs and their more sophisticated cousin, the LSTM. These were architectures designed specifically to handle sequences and they dominated language modeling for the better part of a decade. The fundamental idea of an RNN is elegant. You process a sequence one element at a time, one word at a time, say, and at each step the network produces two things, an output and a hidden state. The hidden state is like a summary of everything the network has seen so far. it gets passed along to the next step. So when the network processes word number five, it has access not just to word five, but to a compressed summary of words 1 through 4. It's like a reader with a notebook jotting down a running summary as they read through a sentence. And the summary at any point reflects everything that came before. This was a real step forward and it opened up possibilities that the earlier approaches simply couldn't touch. RNNs could process variable length sequences. A sentence could be three words or 300 and the same network could handle both. They could in principle capture dependencies between words that were far apart. and they powered significant advances in machine translation, speech recognition, and text generation. Through the late 2000s and early 2010s, Google deployed RNN-based systems for voice search. Apple used them in Siri. Machine translation improved noticeably, but in practice, RNN's struggled with longer sequences. The problem was that hidden state, that notebook. Imagine you have to summarize an entire novel in a single paragraph. Updating that paragraph after every chapter and that one paragraph is all you get to carry forward. Early information gets overwritten. Details get lost. The summary becomes a blur. In technical terms, gradients, the error signals used during training to adjust the network's parameters, tended to either vanish or explode as they were propagated backward through long sequences. A gradient that starts at step 50 and needs to influence learning at step one has to pass through 49 multiplication steps. And if each multiplication makes it a little smaller, by the time it arrives, it's essentially zero. The network can't learn long range connections because the training signal has faded away. The LSTM invented by Sep Hawk Rider and Jurgen Schmidhuber in 1997 at the Technical University of Munich was specifically designed to address this problem and the solution was genuinely clever. Hulk writer had actually diagnosed the vanishing gradient problem in his diploma thesis in 1991 identifying it as the fundamental bottleneck for sequence learning and the LSTM was his answer. The architecture introduced a set of internal gates. Think of them as learned switches that controlled what information to keep, what to forget, and what to output at each step. There's an input gate that decides what new information to let in, a forget gate that decides what old information to throw away, and an output gate that decides what to reveal to the rest of the network. These gates are themselves neural networks trained alongside everything else, so the model learns what's worth remembering and what's not. Think of it like a sophisticated filing system. Instead of a simple notebook, some information gets filed away in long-term storage and protected from being overwritten. Other information is deemed irrelevant and discarded. The cell state, the internal memory of the LSTM, can carry information across many time steps without it decaying because the forget gate can learn to keep it intact. It worked remarkably well. LSTMs were dramatically better than basic RNN's at capturing long range dependencies, and they became the workhorse of sequence modeling for nearly two decades. When Google replaced its old phrase-based translation system with a neural system in 2016, Google neural machine translation or G&T the core was an LSTM network and the improvement in translation quality was so dramatic that the company rolled it out across all its translation pairs almost overnight. But even LSTMs had a fundamental architectural limitation that would eventually prove fatal. And it has to do with that word sequential. An RNN or LSTM processes words one at a time in order. Word one, then word two, then word three. Each step depends on the one before it. You can't skip ahead. You can't fully parallelize it. And that means two things. First, training is slow. If your sequence is a thousand words long, you need a thousand sequential steps and each one has to wait for the previous one to finish. On modern hardware, which is designed for massive parallelism, doing millions of things simultaneously. This sequential bottleneck is painful. Let me tell you what actually happens step by step when you type a question into one of these models and it generates a response because this is the part that most people have never had explained and it's worth understanding in detail. First, your text gets tokenized. The model doesn't actually see words. It sees tokens which are chunks of text that might be whole words, parts of words, or even individual characters. Most modern language models use a tokenization scheme called bite pair encoding or BPE which was originally developed as a data compression algorithm by Philip Gage in 1994 and adapted for neural machine translation by Rico Senrich Barry Hado and Alexandra Burch at the University of Edinburgh in 2015. The idea is that common words like the or is get their own token, but less common words get split into pieces. Understanding might become under and standing. Electromagnetic might become electro and magnet and ick. A word the model has never seen before. A brand name, a madeup word, a term from a language it wasn't primarily trained on can still be represented by splitting it into familiar pieces. This gives the model a vocabulary of typically 30,000 to 100,000 tokens that can represent any possible text, however unusual. Each token gets converted into a vector through the embedding layer, a learned lookup table that maps each token to a dense vector of numbers. Positional information is added and then the whole sequence passes through the transformer layers, one after another. Attention feed forward. Attention feed forward. Layer after layer. Each one refining the representation. Each one allowing the tokens to gather information from each other and build up richer, more contextual representations. At the very end, after the final transformer layer, the model produces for the last position in the sequence a vector that represents its belief about what should come next.

This vector gets projected through one more linear layer into a set of scores, one for each token in the vocabulary. These scores, called logats, go through a softmax function that converts them into probabilities. The model might say there's a 30% chance the next token is the, a 15% chance it's a, a 2% chance it's cat, and so on, for every token in the vocabulary. These probabilities sum to one and they represent the model's best guess given everything it's processed about what comes next. And then comes the choice. This is where temperature and sampling strategies come in. And it's a surprisingly important part of how these systems behave in practice. The most obvious approach would be to just always pick the token with the highest probability. This greedy approach, can tend to produce dull, repetitive text. It's like always ordering your favorite dish at a restaurant. Safe, but boring, and sometimes it leads you into loops where the model repeats itself. Instead, most systems sample from the probability distribution. They pick a token randomly, but weighted by the probabilities. So higher probability tokens are more likely to be chosen, but lower probability ones can still show up. This introduces controlled randomness that makes the output more varied, more natural, more interesting. The temperature parameter controls how much randomness there is. A low temperature close to zero makes the distribution sharper, concentrating almost all the probability on the most likely tokens. The output becomes more predictable, more conservative. A high temperature makes the distribution flatter, giving more chance to unlikely tokens. The output becomes more creative, more surprising, more prone to unusual choices. There are also techniques like top k sampling which only considers the k most likely tokens and nucleus sampling sometimes called top k sampling which considers the smallest set of tokens whose cumulative probability exceeds some threshold p. These techniques prevent the model from choosing extremely unlikely tokens that would make the output nonsensical while still allowing enough randomness for natural sounding text. There's something almost zen about these sampling parameters. The temperature, the top threshold, the nucleus probability, their knobs that control the boundary between order and randomness, between predictability and surprise. Turn the temperature all the way down and you get a rigid, deterministic machine that always says the most obvious thing. Turn it all the way up and you get a creative madness. A word salad that might contain flashes of brilliance buried in nonsense. The art, and it really is more art than science, is finding the sweet spot for each application. A system answering factual questions wants low temperature. A system writing poetry or brainstorming creative ideas wants higher temperature. There's been some interesting experimental work on this. This connects to one of the deepest and most debated questions about large language models. Do these systems understand anything? What we can say is that these models do something, whatever we call it, that produces behavior remarkably similar to understanding in many situations. They can transfer knowledge from one domain to another in ways that seem creative. And yet the same systems also fail in ways that suggest something is missing. They make errors that no one who genuinely understood the subject would make. There's a famous thought experiment from the philosopher John Surl called the Chinese room that's been invoked constantly in these debates. The person looks up the appropriate response in the manual, writes those characters, and slips them back out. From outside, the room appears to understand Chinese. Appropriate answers come back. One of the most exciting frontiers in AI research right now is interpretability. The effort to look inside these models and understand what they're actually doing internally. Because right now a large language model is essentially a black box in a functional sense. We know the input. We know the output. We know the architecture. But we don't know what the intermediate layers have learned to represent. What concepts do the neurons encode? What computations are the attention heads performing? Are there internal structures that correspond to facts or reasoning steps or world models? There has been genuinely exciting progress on this front. Researchers at Anthropic in a series of papers on what they call mechanistic interpretability have developed techniques for identifying features, directions in the model's internal representation space that correspond to interpretable concepts. They found features that activate for specific topics like the Golden Gate Bridge or for abstract properties like whether text is written in a formal register. They've identified circuits, small subnet networks within the larger model that implement specific computations like identifying the subject of a sentence or performing indirect object identification. And in some cases, they've been able to trace the flow of information through the model and understand why it produces a particular output. In one particularly striking piece of work, researchers found that they could intervene on individual features inside the model, amplifying or suppressing them, and observe predictable changes in behavior. If you amplified the Golden Gate Bridge feature, the model would start mentioning the Golden Gate Bridge in conversations where it normally wouldn't and would even start to identify as the Golden Gate Bridge when asked about itself. This kind of causal intervention where you change something inside the model and see the expected effect on the output is strong evidence that the features being identified are genuinely part of the model's computational process, not just correlational artifacts. But we're still in the very early days of this work. Understanding a few features in a model with billions of parameters is like understanding a few neurons in a brain. It's a start, but it's nowhere near a complete picture. And as models get larger and more capable, the challenge of understanding them grows.
